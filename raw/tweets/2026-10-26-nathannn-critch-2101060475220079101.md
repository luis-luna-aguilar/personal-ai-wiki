How to Build a Brain in the AI Age 
48
133K
The people who will dominate the AI age are not learning AI. I know that sounds wrong, when every tech leader, LinkedIn productivity guru, and conference keynote is screaming that "this is a gamechanger!" They say learn the tools, prompts, get certified, the whole shabang. Mark Cuban says if he were young today, he'd spend every spare minute learning AI. 
But I think it's almost entirely wrong. "Learn AI" is the wrong verb attached to the wrong object. I've spent three years watching the consequences of that mistake in school districts, boardrooms, and government offices, and everyone arguing both sides of "ban AI" and "AI helps learning" are missing a critical point.
THE POINT: The people thriving with AI learned how to think first, and the tools just amplified what was already there. The people struggling (the ones who took the courses, got the certifications, and still can't get AI to produce anything that matters) are missing the skill that makes the technology useful: the ability to lever their intelligence to produce value. In the common tech tongue, we can say this is the ability to evaluate, judge, and sit with ambiguity until you "figure it out."
This is a cognitive skill, and one we embarrassingly lack in society today. I want to share a difference I have seen in the field that permeates our young as much as it does our workforce.
There's a concept in developmental psychology that explains nearly every AI success and failure I've witnessed.
Horizontal development is learning new things. New tools, new facts, new skills. It's adding more apps to your phone. Every "master ChatGPT in 30 days" program is horizontal development. To imagine this, you're pouring more water into the same glass.
Vertical development is changing the structure of how you think. Instead of adding water, you're getting a bigger glass. It's the difference between a chess player who memorizes more openings and a chess player who starts seeing the whole board differently.
We now have rigorous evidence of what happens when you get this distinction wrong. The evidence is what moved me to publish this article now because of this style of AI implementation is causing a schism that lacks true understanding and education of this technology.
What I saw in the rooms
I will share a few anecdotes of how how things have played out IRL, then I will share research from those who are more rigorous and empirical than I. 

Anecdote: A school district rolls out AI tools and trains everyone. On paper, adoption looks great. But the 10-15% who were already strong evaluative thinkers do remarkable things with the tools. Everyone else stops using the tools within weeks. The district checks a box to show the board, but the actual cognitive impact is a fraction of what they're claiming.
The same pattern plays out in corporate. This is outdated now, but (Anecdote 2) Harvard Business School tracked BCG consultants, elite professionals at one of the most selective firms on earth, using AI. When the AI was confidently wrong, the consultants who relied on it were 19 percentage points less accurate than those who worked without it. They saw confident output and stopped thinking.
If it's happening to BCG consultants, it's happening everywhere.
But the clearest evidence of what's at stake showed up this year in a study of 27,000 Chinese secondary students (Anecdote 3). Researchers tracked what happened when students gained access to generative AI over a two-year period, without guidance or leadership. Within five months, half of the students who adopted AI had fully outsourced their homework. This means that they stopped doing the mental work entirely.
This led to a 24% decline on high-stakes exam scores. Benjamin Riley, who analyzed the underlying data, calls this the 50 Percent Problem. I highly recommend that read. Half of all students given AI tools used them to automate the exact cognitive processes that learning requires. The homework scores went up and the actual learning went down. AI created what Riley calls a "mental masquerade," performance that looked like progress while the real capacity underneath was eroding.
While everyone is getting up in arms about the negative results, I truly believe that the study did NOT prove that AI is harmful. It proved that AI without cognitive development is harmful. And there is a huge difference between the two. Those students were using AI as a replacement for thinking because NOBODY taught them the difference. Nobody built the cognitive capacity that turns a shortcut into a tool. 

Please indulge this comparison, as I speak through stories. Imagine handing a 15 or 16 year old keys to your car and saying "you can drive!" without any drivers Ed, learning the rules of the road, or passing a drivers test. Of course we wouldn't do that, right? Cars are dangerous and we must teach our youths to be responsible drivers. 

Banning AI from those same young people (or older) is like saying you are not allowed to drive because it is dangerous, and providing no learning opportunity to build a safe driver afterwards. We use cars and recognize the use of cars because of the benefits of autonomy, personal safety, and trust that everyone has been trained to drive. Yes, we still have accidents, but when have accidents caused us to ban driving in a state? We send people to traffic school, and in more severe cases, force a suspended license. We built a process grounded in education and transparency. We are not doing that today with AI. 
It is such an important distinction to make today. A student (or worker) who uses AI to skip the work is outsourcing the one process that builds their brain. A student who uses AI to stress-test their own reasoning, to challenge their assumptions, to push past the limits of what they can hold in their head on their own, that student is using the tool the way it was designed to be used. One produces dependency, the other produces growth, and the independent variable is always the mind operating it.
The skill with no name
So what does this mind have to do to operate AI properly? In the science, they call it metacognition, or thinking about your own thinking. 
This is the ability to receive a confident, polished answer from a machine and ask: Is this actually right? Is this the right question to begin with? What's missing that the machine wouldn't know to include?
It's the difference between asking AI to write your strategy and asking AI to stress-test the strategy you already built. You can be a passenger or a pilot.
Our education system, from kindergarten through college, was specifically designed to produce passengers. But I won't get into that now. 
Even the "innovators" are getting this wrong
This is where I'm going to make some people uncomfortable. Because the schools that claim to be reinventing education for the AI age are making the exact same mistake in a shinier package.
Take Alpha School. Their model compresses core academics into two hours a day using AI, then frees the rest of the day for projects and real-world activities. The efficiency is impressive, but look at what's happening in those two hours. Students are still moving through a fixed curriculum, just faster, because a machine is feeding it to them at the right pace. That's horizontal development on a treadmill set to 2x speed. You didn't change the exercise to build better muscle, you just changed the clock.
Nobody is asking: are we developing the capacity of these students to think above the system they're operating in? Or are we just producing the same cognitive profile and celebrating that it takes two hours instead of seven?
Building a brain in the AI age is about developing the cognitive capacity that makes AI useful instead of dangerous. That means three things:
First, it means getting comfortable with confusion. Every major leap in how a person thinks requires a period where your old way of seeing things stops working and the new way hasn't arrived yet. That gap feels like failure, but it's actually growth. The people who avoid that discomfort stay stuck, while the people who lean into it level up.
Second, it means practicing judgment instead of production. The AI can produce, and our job is becoming to judge. If you spend all day consuming AI output without critically evaluating it, that muscle atrophies. If you spend even 20 minutes a day deliberately questioning, stress-testing, and pushing back on what a machine gives you, it grows.
Third, it means asking different questions. Not "how do I use this tool?" because that's horizontal. Rather, "what is this tool failing to see?" That's vertical. Not "how do I get better output?" But "am I even solving the right problem?" 
If you take one idea from this post, take this:
Stop trying to learn AI. Start learning how to think.
Your ability to hold complexity, to sit with ambiguity (and find a path through it), is what appreciates in value every single time the machines get smarter.
Cognitive scientist Dan Willingham put it as clearly as anyone: "AI tools should not substitute for tasks where students would benefit from doing the mental work themselves. Only use AI for what you already know how to do." Think about that. Do the mental work, build your own Cognitive Architecture first. Then, you will be one to employ a powerful tool to your benefit to stretch your cognitive abilities, not replace them.
Everyone is telling you to learn the tools. I'm telling you to build the brain that makes the tools worth using. If this work interests you, that is my entire mission! TO empower people to be the best versions of themselves, mentally and physically. I hope everyone takes this opportunity to build their own cognitive abilities, so they can amplify them with the tools available. That is the way forward, for all of us.
Nathan Critchett is the author of Cognitive Architecture: How to Think When Machines Think For You. He's a member of the Los Angeles County Fire Department and has spent three years studying the gap between AI capability and human cognitive capacity across school districts and organizations nationwide.
