# How to turn AI into a whole team that runs your business

*By Yarchi (@undefinedKi), September 4, 2026 — marked "Paid Partnership," sponsored by Viktor.com*

Most advice about running a business alone is about doing less. This is the opposite. It is about the work you already know needs doing and never get to, and how to put it on rails so it happens without you starting it every time.

I built the whole thing in an afternoon and ran it against real files. Here is the setup, what came back, and the parts that surprised me.

What this covers: splitting the business into lanes, writing the context once, drawing the approval line, briefing each lane (with five you can copy), putting it on a schedule, what came back on the first day, and the same shape at Uber, Spotify and Stripe.

Five steps, in order. The first three do not involve AI at all, they are just Slack and a folder of files, and that is deliberate. Most of the work sits in steps two and three, they are the two people skip, and skipping them is why these setups fall apart in the second week.

## Step 1. Split the business into lanes

The mistake is one assistant with one enormous prompt, expected to do everything. What holds up is the opposite: one lane, one job, one place it lives.

For a services business, five lanes cover almost everything:

- **Clients.** Who is at what stage, what ships this week, what is blocked and who it waits on.
- **Leads.** Research on companies that fit, and first touch drafts. Nothing sends from here.
- **Delivery.** The actual work product, whatever that is for you.
- **Reporting.** Numbers pulled from your platforms, drafted for review.
- **Finance.** Draft invoices, and a weekly summary of what is issued, paid and overdue.

Each lane gets its own channel in Slack or Teams. Not folders, tabs or channels — a channel already has a history, and the history is context nobody has to re-explain.

## Step 2. Write the context once

This is the part that decides whether any of it works, and it is the part everyone skips.

Every lane gets one pinned message. Think of it as the job description you would write for a person doing that job, if you had to write it down instead of explaining it over three weeks.

Four things go in it: what we sell and to who (one or two lines — everything downstream depends on this); what this lane delivers (named outputs, not areas of responsibility); what gets escalated (the situations where the answer is stop and ask); and the rules that never bend (short, five or six lines maximum).

Here is the finance one from my setup, unedited:

> Retainers are billed on the first of the month. Project work is billed on delivery. Amounts and terms are in the client base in Drive.
> Create draft invoices here and post them for review. Never finalise, never send, never mark anything as paid.
> Every Friday at 16:00 post a summary: invoices issued, invoices paid, invoices overdue with days outstanding, month to date total. Under 10 lines.
> Flag anything overdue past 14 days separately at the top, with the client name and the amount.

Notice the shape: facts, deliverable, cadence, then the limit. Every lane follows it, and the last paragraph is always a limit.

**The rulings file.** Alongside the pinned messages, keep one file where every correction lands as a single line — not a document you write once, a running list. When you correct something, the correction goes there, and every lane reads it before working. Mine has lines like: "Cost per booked consultation is the metric, not cost per lead," "No campaign is ever built unpaused, on any account, for any reason," "Reports carry the source and date range for every figure, or the figure does not go in." That file is how a correction becomes permanent instead of repeated. It is the single highest leverage thing in the whole setup and it costs you one line at a time.

## Step 3. Draw the approval line, and never move it

Some work is reversible and some is not. Sort every action into those two piles, once, and the rest of the setup becomes simple.

Runs without asking: reading, researching, drafting, summarising, building something in a paused state. If it is wrong you delete it.

Waits for you: sending, publishing, spending, finalising, going live. If it is wrong you are apologising to a client.

In practice this means three of my five channels say, in the pinned message, that nothing leaves them. The leads channel has no sending tool connected at all, which is deliberate. The rule is easier to keep when the capability is not there.

## Step 4. Brief each lane like a colleague

Context is standing. A brief is the specific ask. Same pattern in all five: context first, named deliverable, a cadence, and a gate on anything that touches money or the outside world.

## Step 5. Put it on a schedule

The difference between a tool and a lane is that a lane wakes up on its own.

A schedule is one sentence in a brief. "Every workday at 8:00" is the whole configuration. Once that is set, the standup arrives before you open the laptop, and your morning starts with a decision instead of a question.

Order the schedules so they can read each other. The standup runs at 7:55 and the rollup at 8:00, because the rollup pulls from the channel the standup posts into. Five minutes apart, and the sequence holds.

Three schedules cover most of a services business: a morning rollup across every lane, a standup inside the lane with deadlines, and a money summary on Friday afternoon.

## What came back when the AI employee ran it

I ran this on Viktor, an AI employee who joins Slack or Teams as a member of the workspace and connects to the tools the business already runs on. Setup was one afternoon.

Five things from the first day are worth reporting, and three of them are refusals: Viktor would not invent data he was missing (before I connected Drive, he checked, found nothing, and posted nothing rather than a plausible-looking status board); he would not answer a question the data could not support (asked why cost per booking rose, he explained the data couldn't answer that and reported two different candidate numbers rather than picking one); he applied a rule nobody reminded him about (a buried note about a client's budget-cap question, surfaced unprompted in an unrelated summary); he delivered finished work, not chat (a typeset PDF report opening with a "three limits on this month's data" section); and he built a working dashboard at a URL, labeled "snapshot, not auto-updating" without being asked to add that label.

## This is the same shape large teams landed on

None of the above is specific to a one person business. It is the small version of what companies with thousands of engineers arrived at independently: different scales, same three ideas — one job per agent, context that lives outside the conversation, and a gate on anything irreversible. The structure was never the hard part. Having someone to run it was.

## The build sheet

Save this. You run it once per lane: one channel per lane, never one assistant for everything; one pinned message per channel; one rulings file; sort actions once (reversible runs, irreversible waits); where a rule matters, remove the capability rather than relying on the rule; schedules before prompts; next lane only when the last one produced without you starting it.
